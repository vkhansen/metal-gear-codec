Hard start. You taught that in the propulsion course.

**BIG BOSS**
I did. I'll say it again, because the people who need to hear it are about to stand next to the article.

In a rocket, a start is "hard" when propellant is allowed into the chamber *before* a controlled flame is established. Unreacted oxidiser — and, if present, fuel vapour — fills the free volume. When ignition finally occurs, that inventory burns as a near-constant-volume chemical explosion. Not a choked, nozzle-limited motor. Chamber pressure spikes far above the maximum expected operating pressure.

Typical results: failed bolts. Ruptured case. Blown injector or nozzle. Fragments.

Karabeyoglu et al. treat this as an elementary, preventable failure mode.

**JEERASAK**
And on a hybrid?

**BIG BOSS**
A hybrid already has its fuel in the chamber. The solid grain. The port. The head-end cavity. The nozzle volume. Only the oxidiser is valved.

A correct start has a strict sequence. An igniter — torch, pyro, or equivalent — must pyrolyse the grain surface *first*. So that when N₂O arrives, it burns at the fuel surface in a boundary-layer flame. The chamber never holds a large unreacted fill.

If that sequence is inverted, or simply omitted — N₂O flow with no established flame — the port and free volume fill with oxidiser. In an N₂O hybrid that fill is especially dangerous. N₂O is itself a monopropellant. Mixed with fuel vapour from a warm or soaked grain, igniter products, or hydrocarbon contamination, it becomes a premixed charge.

Lighting that charge is not "starting the motor."

It is detonating a vessel.

The hybrid's usual safety argument — fuel and oxidiser stored separately — does not apply once oxidiser has been admitted to a grain-filled chamber with no flame.
